Support, advisory and workshops

Depend on it with someone behind you.

Cratis is MIT licensed and always will be. A support plan isn't access to the software — it's an ongoing relationship with the people who build it, and a written answer to the questions your architecture review will ask.

First, the question you were going to ask anyway.

What happens if you stop?

Everything you depend on is open source and public. It runs on databases you already operate, speaks open standards, and works from several programming languages. There is no licence server, nothing phoning home, and no proprietary format holding your data.

If Cratis ceased to operate tomorrow, you would keep all of it — the software, the data, and the right to continue it yourself. Nothing to renew, nothing to migrate off.

Every support plan additionally guarantees a ninety-day wind-down with handover documentation.

We think that makes us a lower-lock-in choice than most vendors ten times our size. It's certainly a more honest one.

What "advisory hours" means

Not just fixing things when they break. Design reviews, second opinions before a decision gets expensive to reverse, migration planning, and the architectural questions that keep people awake.

What a critical incident means

Something is broken in production and it is our software's fault. Response times are measured to a real human answer — not an automated acknowledgement.

Questions

You're two people. What happens if you stop?

Fair question, and the honest answer is better than the reassuring one.

Everything is open source and developed in public. It runs on your own infrastructure, on databases you already operate. There is no licence server and no proprietary format holding your data. If we stopped tomorrow, you would keep everything you are running and the right to continue it yourself.

That is a stronger position than most teams have with most vendors, and it is deliberate. Support plans additionally include a ninety-day wind-down with handover documentation.

We are small, and we intend to stay small enough that you can always reach the person who wrote the code.

Is this more than we need?

Sometimes, yes — and we will tell you when. It earns its keep wherever decisions have consequences later: payments, claims, contracts, anything an auditor or a customer might ask about. For a settings screen or a simple lookup table, it is more than you need, and the platform works perfectly well without it.

We already have something that works. Why change?

Then you may not need us, and we will say so. If what you have works, your team is productive, and nobody is asking questions the system cannot answer — stay where you are.

Organisations come to us when the design lives in a few people's heads, when the pieces have drifted apart, or when a question about the past takes a week to answer. That is the part we built.

What about lock-in?

Concretely: the core is open source and public, it runs on your infrastructure, on databases you already have, from several programming languages. You can leave whenever you want. We would rather you did not want to.

How does this actually help with AI?

Three specific things, not a slogan. Assistants are taught your conventions directly. Automated checks reject work that drifts off them, before a human ever reviews it. And an assistant can ask the running system what happened, in plain language.

That last one is read-only by design. An assistant can look, but changing anything still goes through the same path as everything else. The record stays honest.

How long is a version supported?

We are writing this down formally. Until it is published, ask us directly and you will get a straight answer about the version you are on.
